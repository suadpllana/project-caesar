`/app` is the statement executor of a single-session relational store, cut down to the part that decides what each statement of a transaction reports. `/app/run_tx.py` takes a program file and prints one line per statement. `/app/progs` holds five programs.

A program declares its tables and constraints first, one per line. `table T k c1 c2 ...` is a table whose first column is its integer key. `check C T col notnull` is violated by a row whose col is null, and `check C T col min N` by a row whose col is below N. A null is never below anything. `fk C T col P action` is violated by a row of T whose col is not null and matches the key of no row of P, and a key k is left behind by it when P has no row k and some row of T holds k in col. The action is `cascade`, `setnull`, `restrict` or `noaction`. A check or fk line may end in `deferrable`, which lets `set` move the constraint between immediate and deferred, or in `deferrable deferred`, which also starts it deferred. Any other constraint is always immediate. Declaration order is the order of the check and fk lines. `row T k v ...` lines give the committed rows, and every value is an integer or `-` for null. Committed rows break no constraint. No table refers to itself, restrict keys are never deferrable, no constraint is called `key` or `all`, and an update never names a key column.

The statements are `begin`, `commit`, `rollback`, `savepoint S`, `release S`, `rollback to S`, `set C deferred`, `set C immediate`, `set all deferred`, `set all immediate`, `insert T k v ...`, `update T k col v [col v ...]` and `delete T k`. `begin` only comes outside a transaction. Everything else comes only inside one. An insert of a key that is already there raises `key` on it at once. An update or a delete of a key that is not there does nothing.

Writing a row, by an insert, an update or `setnull`, checks it at once against each immediate check of its table in declaration order, and the first one it violates raises. Deleting a row removes it and then goes over the fks whose parent is its table, in declaration order and whatever their modes. For each, it takes the rows that hold the deleted key in that fk's column at that moment, in increasing key order: `cascade` deletes each one still there in this same way before moving on, `setnull` writes null into that column, `restrict` raises at once on the deleted row if there is any such row, and `noaction` does nothing. When the writes are over, each immediate fk in declaration order looks at the rows of its table the statement wrote that are still there and at the keys of its parent the statement deleted, in the order the statement first wrote or deleted each. The first row that violates it or key it leaves behind raises. A statement that raises leaves nothing behind. It prints `raise C T k`, naming the constraint that raised and the table and key of the row, or of the key left behind, that it raised on. Then the transaction is aborted.

A deferred constraint is owed instead. The transaction keeps a ledger with at most one entry for each constraint and row, or constraint and key left behind, printed as the constraint, the table and the key. The ledger is ordered by declaration and, within a constraint, by when each entry was made. When a statement ends without raising, each deferred constraint, in declaration order, looks at the rows of its table the statement wrote or deleted and, for an fk, at the keys of its parent the statement deleted or inserted, in the order the statement first wrote or deleted each. Each row or key it looks at that is a violation now and has no entry gets one, and each that has an entry and is no violation now loses it. An entry that is still a violation stays where it stands. A statement changes no other entry, so a row mended by something done elsewhere, such as its missing key being inserted, keeps its entry until a statement looks at it.

`set C immediate` goes over C's entries in ledger order and `set all immediate` over every entry. An entry is still a violation when its row violates the constraint, or its key is left behind, at that moment. If one is, the first such raises and nothing changes. Otherwise they all go and the constraints become immediate. `commit` does the same over every entry, and when it raises the whole transaction is rolled back. `set C deferred` and `set all deferred` only change modes. In any `set`, `all` means every deferrable constraint, and naming one that is not deferrable is an error. Each transaction starts with the declared modes.

`rollback to S` goes back to the latest savepoint named S. The rows, the ledger and every mode are put back exactly as they stood when that savepoint was made, each entry in its old place, the savepoints made after it are dropped, S stays, and an aborted state ends. `release S` drops the latest S and every savepoint made after it. Naming a savepoint that does not exist is an error. An error prints `error` and aborts the transaction. In an aborted transaction every statement other than `rollback`, `rollback to S` and `commit` prints `aborted` and does nothing, and `commit` rolls back and prints `rollback`.

Every other line is `ok`, then the entries the statement took out of the ledger as `-C T k` in the order they stood, then the ones it put in as `+C T k` in ledger order, all separated by single spaces. An entry in the ledger both before and after a statement is in neither list, even if its place has changed. A commit or a rollback that ends the transaction takes out every entry.
