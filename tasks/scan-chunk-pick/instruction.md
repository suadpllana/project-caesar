`/app` is the scan layer of a columnar store, cut down to the part that decides which pages get read. A segment file holds one table, the changes made to it since it was written, and the queries to run over it. `/app/run_scan.py` takes one and prints what happens. `seg G N K` opens the file with the statistics granularity, the row count and the column count. Rows and columns are numbered from 0. A column is stored as chunks and a chunk as pages, both in row order and together covering every row; chunks are numbered from 0 within their column and pages from 0 within their chunk, and the partitions of two columns need not line up. `ch C p` opens a plain chunk of column C, and `ch C d m e1 ... em` opens one with a dictionary of m entries, ascending. Each `pg n u mn mx x s f ...` line after it is a page of that chunk: n rows, u of them null, mn and mx the recorded low and high over the rest, x either `e` or `w`, s the sum of the rest, and f either `v` followed by the n values or `i` followed by n indexes into the dictionary of the chunk, `-` for a null either way. Only a `d` chunk has `i` pages. `up C r v` says row r now holds v in column C, `-` for a null, and `del r` says row r was deleted; a row has at most one update per column. `qry` opens a query, `prd` carries one condition, `prj` names the columns to report and `end` closes it. A condition is `ge C v`, `le C v`, `eq C v` or `ne C v` against a value, or `nn C` and `nu C` for not-null and null. A null satisfies `nu` and nothing else. Every query names at least one condition and at least one column. `/app/segs` holds four segment files.

The rest of the tree is replaced by our own copy before a segment file is run, including any new file put beside those six. `/app/run_scan.py` calls `live.fresh` once per file and then `live.start`, `pick.run`, `live.rows` and `proj.run` for each query, so those five keep their names and the arguments they are handed.

Pages are never rewritten. A row's value in a column is its update when it has one, and what its page holds otherwise. A deleted row is never alive. Everything a page says about itself describes it as written: its header and the counts a read of it yields. Deleted rows and replaced values are included.

A page header knows three things: u of its rows are null, every other value lies between its bounds, and those values sum to s. Under `e` the bounds are the recorded pair. Under `w` that pair was rounded inward to a multiple of G, so the bounds are the recorded low less G-1 and the recorded high plus G-1. A page whose rows are all null writes both as `-` and has no bounds. What the first two facts prove about every row a page holds, its header settles: that a condition fails for all of them, or holds for all of them. Every non-null value on an `i` page is one of the entries of its chunk. So the dictionary can settle a comparison for an `i` page the same way. No entry satisfying it fails every row; every entry satisfying it, with the page holding no null, passes every row. It says nothing about a `v` page and never settles `nn` or `nu`. How many entries a dictionary has, and which pages are `i`, is known without consulting it.

Reading a page prints `dc` with its column, chunk and page numbers. Consulting the dictionary of a chunk prints `rd` with its column and chunk. A file is scanned with one memory. A page read or a dictionary consulted by any query is known to every query after it in the file, and each prints only the first time. Nothing is consulted or read while something cheaper already answers. A header costs nothing. So does a page already read. A dictionary costs its charge, and a page costs a read. A page is only ever read, or its dictionary consulted, for a live row that takes its value in that column from that page. When a condition is applied to a chunk, its live rows that carry an update in that column are tested on that value. Then its pages are taken in order. The other live rows of each page are settled by its header if it can, then by a read already made; otherwise, for a comparison on an `i` page, the dictionary is consulted, however little it turns out to settle, before the page is read. Rows the condition fails die.

Reading a page settles the exact count of every condition of the query over that column on that page, counted over the page as written. A page read before a query starts counts exactly from its start. Otherwise the count is the spread of the header. Take two widths. One is the width of the bounds, the high less the low plus one; the other is the width of the part the condition asks for. That part is the high less v plus one for `ge v`, v less the low plus one for `le v`, and one for `eq v` and `ne v` when v lies within the bounds and none when it does not. Where it comes out at nothing or less the count is zero for `ge v`, `le v` and `eq v`, and all of the non-null rows of the page for `ne v`. Otherwise cut the part down to the width of the bounds, multiply the non-null rows by it, divide by that width and round up; that is the count for `ge v`, `le v` and `eq v`, and for `ne v` it is the non-null rows less it. `nn` counts the non-null rows, `nu` counts the nulls, and a page with no bounds counts nothing for anything but `nu`. The count of a condition on a chunk is the sum of its counts on the pages of that chunk.

A condition and a chunk of its column are a pending pair while the condition has not been applied to that chunk and the chunk still holds a live row. The pair applied next is always the one expected to leave the fewest rows alive. That is the smaller of the live rows that chunk holds and the count of the condition on it. A tie goes to the condition written earlier in the query, then to the lower chunk number. The conditions are done when nothing is pending.

Then comes `sel`, the number of live rows and a digest of them: start at zero and, for each live row in ascending order, multiply by 1000003, add the row number plus one and take the remainder by 2305843009213693951. After it every column the query names is reported, in the order it names them and once for each time it names it, as `prj`, the column, the count of live rows whose value there is not null, and the sum of those values. What that line needs from a page is how many of the live rows taking their value from it hold a non-null value and what those values sum to. Before the line, every page of the column holding such a row, in chunk and page order, has it answered on the same terms: by a page already read, or by its header when every row of the page is null, when the page holds no null and its bounds are one value, or when those rows are every row of the page; then by consulting the dictionary, for an `i` page holding no null when the dictionary has a single entry; and by a read otherwise. `qry` and the number of the query, counted from 0 within the file, opens each query, and nothing else is printed. Every query starts with every row not deleted alive.
